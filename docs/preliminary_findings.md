# Preliminary findings, 14 November 2026

This note reports what the dataset of this repository shows so far about the data-centre capacity outside mainland China in which Chinese firms hold a role, and about the rules that cover the AI chips in that capacity. Every figure below comes from a row of the dataset, and every row cites the page that supports the row, a passage that a script has found on that page and a grade of the evidence for the row.

## What the dataset holds

The dataset records 80 sites in 17 countries and territories, with 103 roles held by 21 firms and funds, and 55 public cloud regions of Alibaba Cloud, Huawei Cloud, Tencent Cloud and BytePlus. The dataset also records 13 reports of AI chips that Chinese firms reach outside China, 25 figures of capacity at sites, 9 rows on the largest customers of 5 operators, which give the share of revenue from each customer or name the customer, 20 rules, bills, guidance notes, reviews, prosecutions, contracts and requirements of suppliers that bear on those chips, and censuses of the 392 data-centre facilities that a public list names in Johor, Batam, Greater Bangkok, Singapore and Greater Kuala Lumpur. The 725 rows carry 771 passages, and all 771 were found on their pages. 197 of the 725 rows rest on a primary source, which is the firm that the row concerns, another firm that holds a role at the site or signed the agreement, or a public authority, 506 rest on a secondary source such as a news outlet or a commercial directory, and 22 are unconfirmed. This note describes version 0.2.0 of the dataset, and `CHANGELOG.md` lists the changes since version 0.1.0, which the public post describes.

## Findings

**1. Chinese firms are reported to be the largest customers of three operators outside China.** DayOne, which was a subsidiary of the Chinese operator GDS until 31 December 2024, states in its registration statement at the SEC that its largest customer, which it describes as a global technology company with a leading short-form video platform, provided 69.4 percent of its revenue in 2025 and 69.2 percent in the first half of 2026. Bloomberg reported in July 2025 that this customer is ByteDance. The British operator Nscale states in its own registration statement that its largest customer provided 73 percent of its revenue in 2025, and Tom's Hardware, citing the Financial Times, reports that this customer is a Singapore subsidiary of ByteDance. The Japanese operator Datasection received essentially all its AI infrastructure revenue in the year to March 2026, through one intermediary, from an end customer that it does not name, and the Financial Times has reported that this customer is Tencent.

**2. Chinese firms reach US chips outside China mainly by renting them.** In 9 of the 13 reports of AI chips, a Chinese firm rents the use of chips that another firm holds. In the other 4, the firm owns the chips: Megaspeed in 1 report, and Bitdeer, a Singapore firm that was spun off from the Beijing firm Bitmain in 2021, in 3. The largest reported numbers of chips are about 100,000 advanced AI chips that Tencent leases at several Oracle data centres in Southeast Asia, as the Financial Times reported in October 2026, about 36,000 Nvidia B200 chips that ByteDance is set to reach through the cloud operator Aolani in Malaysia, and 2,304 Nvidia B200 chips that ByteDance's Singapore subsidiary rents at Nscale's data centre in Glomfjord, Norway. The last case rests on a loan agreement that Nscale filed at the SEC, which gives the number of chips and names the customer.

**3. The rules recorded so far control the shipment of the chips and not their use through a cloud service.** US export rules control exports and transfers of advanced chips, and access to the chips through a cloud service is not a violation of them. Guidance of May 2026 requires a licence for advanced chips destined for any entity whose ultimate parent is in China or Macau, wherever the entity is. The Remote Access Security Act, which would extend US export rules to access through a cloud service, passed the House by 369 votes to 22 on 12 January 2026 and has not passed the Senate. Malaysia has required a permit since July 2025 for exports, transshipments and transits of US-origin AI chips, and its trade ministry stated in June 2025 that servers with Nvidia and other AI chips are not controlled goods in Malaysia and that data centres may decide whom to serve within Malaysian law. The European Union extended its control list on exports to certain advanced computing integrated circuits in November 2025. A draft US rule that would bar the renting of Nvidia chips to Chinese AI firms through servers in Thailand and Singapore was reported in August 2026, and the Department of Commerce has not published the draft. The controls on remote use found so far are private. The loan agreement for the chips at Glomfjord requires Nscale to monitor how its customer uses them for signs of a breach of export control law and to report such signs to the lender. Nvidia is also reported to require new cloud partners in Southeast Asia to enrol in a monitoring system and to have instructed its partners to sell at least 51 percent of their services to non-Chinese customers, two reports that the dataset grades unconfirmed.

**4. Chinese ownership reaches beyond the firms usually counted as Chinese operators.** Global Switch, which owns and operates 12 completed data centres in Amsterdam, Frankfurt, Hong Kong, London, Madrid, Paris and Singapore and 4 under development, states in its annual report for 2025 that Jiangsu Shagang Group holds an indirect stake of 51.8 percent and controls the group. A subsidiary of Shagang holds a service agreement for 15 MW in the Hong Kong data centre until the end of 2027, and the report does not say who uses that capacity. GDS still holds 19.4 percent of DayOne.

**5. A Chinese firm or a fund of a Chinese state lender holds a role at 52 of the 392 facilities in five markets.** Baxtel's public lists name 84 data-centre facilities in Johor, 20 in Batam, 88 in Greater Bangkok, 110 in Singapore and 90 in Greater Kuala Lumpur. A role is recorded at 25 of the 84 in Johor, 6 of the 20 in Batam, 6 of the 88 in Greater Bangkok, 11 of the 110 in Singapore and 4 of the 90 in Greater Kuala Lumpur. A Chinese link is reported but not confirmed at 7 more facilities, and no source found names one at the other 333. In Johor, listed Chinese firms operate, build or plan 17 facilities, among them 14 buildings of DayOne's Nusajaya Tech Park, ByteDance is the anchor tenant of 4 buildings of Bridge Data Centres' MY06 site, and a subsidiary of Megaspeed completed the purchase of the land of a further site in September 2026. In Batam, DayOne operates 4 facilities, and RangeIDC, a data-centre firm listed in Shenzhen, backs the developer of a data-centre site on 30 hectares valued at US$5 billion. In Greater Bangkok, Thailand's Board of Investment approved a data centre of Beijing Haoyang with an IT load of 300 MW and a data centre of an affiliate of zData with an IT load of about 203 MW, both in Rayong, and China Mobile bought the operator of a data centre in Chonburi in March 2025. In Singapore, Global Switch operates 2 facilities and owns the building in which 4 other operators lease their sites, and Singapore selected a proposal of a consortium of AirTrunk and ByteDance in July 2023. In Greater Kuala Lumpur, Bitdeer holds data-centre services agreements for three facilities in Cyberjaya with 71.5 MW of IT load in all, and a subsidiary of DayOne agreed in August 2026 to buy 78.80 acres near Dengkil. The China-ASEAN Investment Cooperation Fund II, whose main anchor sponsor is China Eximbank, invested in June 2026 in Racks Central, which has facilities on the lists of Johor, Batam and Singapore.

**6. The capacity in service that the dataset records comes to between 1,413 MW and 1,652 MW.** The estimate counts each data centre once and takes the figures that DayOne and Global Switch report by market or for the whole firm in place of the figures of their sites. The low estimate counts only figures from primary sources, at sites where the role of the Chinese firm is not unconfirmed and the status of the site is known, and the high estimate counts every figure and the estimates of Epoch AI where Epoch's estimates are larger. Capacity under construction or planned comes to between 4,818 MW and 5,912 MW, of which each estimate counts only the part of a planned total that is not yet in service. DayOne holds most of the capacity: 962 MW in service, 1,328 MW under construction and 2,308 MW of secured powered land in 10 markets, or 4,597 MW in all, and Johor holds 789 MW of the 962 MW in service. 36 of the 79 sites that the estimate covers have no figure of capacity, among them the data centres of Alibaba Cloud, Tencent Cloud and Huawei Cloud, so both estimates understate the capacity of the sites recorded. Epoch AI estimates 1,210 MW of IT power at the 4 sites of this dataset that Epoch's database also holds, and 28 of the 80 sites have an announced capacity of at least 20 MW or a report of AI chips, which places them at level B of the codebook.

## What the dataset adds to existing data

Epoch AI's database of AI data centres holds 95 data centres, 77 of them in the United States and 5 in mainland China, and 4 of the other 13 are sites in this dataset. Epoch estimates the IT power and the chips of each site from satellite imagery, but it does not record the role that each Chinese firm holds at a site or the rules that cover the site. This dataset records both, together with the ownership links and the dependence of operators on Chinese customers. The map of the Australian Strategic Policy Institute's project Mapping China's Tech Giants, relaunched on 8 June 2021 with more than 3,800 entries on 27 Chinese technology firms, includes data centres among the overseas activities of those firms but records neither the capacity of each data centre nor the rules that cover the data centre. Noah Tan's paper for the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace of 5 May 2026 analyses the options for controlling access to AI chips through a cloud service but does not build a dataset of sites. A third part of this dataset, on government data centres financed by Chinese state lenders, is held back until an article on those data centres has been published.

## Method

A person writes each row with the page that supports the row, a passage copied from that page and a grade of the evidence: primary when the firm, another firm at the site or a public authority states the value, secondary when a third party states the value, and unconfirmed when the source rests on unnamed people or the firms have not confirmed the value. A script downloads every page, including annual reports in PDF and filings at the SEC, and counts the passage as found when at least 90 percent of its overlapping pieces of eight characters are on the page. Every figure in this note is a value of a checked row or a count of checked rows, apart from the counts of Epoch's database, which come from Epoch's own file. The tests run on each push, and the dashboard at [newlivehung123123.github.io/overseas_data_centres_China](https://newlivehung123123.github.io/overseas_data_centres_China/) shows every row with its passage and the result of its check.

## Limits

Outside the four markets of the censuses, the dataset records the sites that searches found, and these are not every data centre in which a Chinese firm holds a role. In the four markets each census rests on a commercial directory, because no public register of data centres was found, and a facility at which no Chinese link was found may still have a Chinese tenant that no source names. The web searches behind the censuses of November 2026 were made by Chinese firm and by group of operators, and not for every operator by name. Several reports rest on unnamed sources cited by the Wall Street Journal, the Financial Times or Bloomberg, and the firms have not confirmed them, so the dataset grades these reports unconfirmed and gives the reason in the note of each row. Filings describe their largest customers without naming them, so the names come from press reports. The estimates of Epoch AI are models and are kept apart from the capacity that firms announce, and enter only the high estimate of the capacity estimate. The low and high estimates add figures of IT capacity, of power and of an unspecified basis as the sources state them, although 5,610 MW of the 6,230 MW of the low estimate are IT capacity.

## Next steps

The next step is to search the tenants of the 333 facilities in the five markets at which no Chinese link was found, operator by operator, and to settle the 7 reported links. The study would then take censuses of Jakarta, where the same list names 104 facilities, and of the Gulf and Europe, add satellite imagery, permits and power allocations for the largest sites, record the rules of every host country, find primary sources for the 1,334 MW that only the high estimate counts, and publish the file of government data centres.
